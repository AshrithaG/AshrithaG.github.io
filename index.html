Research

The measurement keeps disagreeing

Most of my work has the same shape. I build a system carefully enough to trust it, then I build the instrument that measures it, and then the instrument disagrees with what the field already believes.

The first time this happened I assumed I had a bug. I had implemented speculative decoding from scratch and it was slower than not using it, at every window size I tried. There was a real problem, but it was not in my code: in eager PyTorch the draft model costs 0.649 of a target forward pass, which pushes break-even past any acceptance rate the draft actually reaches. Then I found the part that mattered more. The lossless guarantee, the property that makes speculative decoding safe to ship, fails in bf16. With draft and target sharing weights, 5 of 480 tokens flipped their argmax against 0 in fp32, traced to the median logit gap between batched verification and sequential decoding.

After that I started looking on purpose, and it kept turning up. ROUGE-L, the metric most query-rewriting work reports, ranks seven rewriting systems backwards from the retrieval they exist to improve, Kendall tau between −0.73 and −0.87 across 12,561 turns. vLLM’s determinism fix works exactly as documented and costs 54 to 67% of throughput, a price the documentation acknowledges but never quantifies, and the cause is batch composition rather than batch size. A reinforcement-learning policy of my own scored 85% on its metrics and 0% on camera, from seed and protocol leakage I had introduced myself. In reasoning-augmented image generation, the compositional failures the field attributes to the decoder turn out to sit in the planner. And in an industry deployment I found an auto-accept gate set at 0.85 that semantic-only scoring had made mathematically unreachable, before it shipped.

The one I care about most is the replay gap. Static replay is how model switching in LLM agents is almost always evaluated: take a recorded trajectory, ask a different model what it would have done at each step, score the answer. It misscores 92 to 97% of those decisions, because it grades each one against a world state the agent never reaches. Showing that meant building the counterfactual: a resumable harness that forks live SWE-bench trajectories mid-run, rebuilds every Docker environment, and continues the fork under a different model. 889 rollouts, 20,141 requests, 42.5 GPU-hours, with both quantized models served co-resident on a single 24 GB card.

That last detail is why the systems half of this work is not a separate interest. You cannot fork and rebuild hundreds of live agent environments, or fit two models and a 28k context onto one consumer GPU, unless you can also write the kernels and the infrastructure underneath. So I do: a C++20 inference engine with ARM NEON depthwise convolution and an int8 CUDA GEMM ladder reaching 122.8 TOPS, and three years of production telemetry systems at Cisco with the on-call pager that came with them.

What I want to work on next is the consequential version of this problem. A model that answers a question wrong has given a bad answer. An agent that acts wrong has changed the world it is being measured in, and nearly every evaluation method we have quietly assumes it has not. Closing that gap is the difference between an agent that benchmarks well and one you can actually deploy.
